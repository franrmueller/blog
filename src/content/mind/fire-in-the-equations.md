*The oldest question is why there is something rather than nothing. It's usually filed as unanswerable, which is a polite way of not asking it. Two ideas I've carried around separately for years turn out to meet exactly there.*

One is from philosophy: that consciousness is not something matter produces but something matter is. The other is from physics: that the equation describing a quantum system never, by itself, produces a definite outcome. Held apart they are two respectable puzzles. Held together they describe a single thing, prior to the universe, whose attention is what makes the universe concrete rather than merely possible. That is as close as I've come to an argument for God, and I'd rather lay it out plainly than dress it up.

Here are the two ideas, the join, and what the join does and doesn't buy you.

## What matter is

Physics describes matter by what it does. Mass is how a thing resists being pushed; charge is how it couples to a field; spin is how it behaves under rotation. Every quantity in the standard model is defined by its relations to other quantities. What stands in those relations, the thing that has the mass and carries the charge, physics does not say, and it never needed to. Galileo got the whole enterprise moving in 1623 by deciding that colour, taste and warmth live in the perceiver and only shape, number and motion live in bodies. Take the qualities out, keep what can be measured, and the world becomes tractable.

Russell noticed in 1927 what that decision leaves behind. We know the structure of matter down to a fine grain and nothing about its intrinsic nature; physics is a map of relations with blanks where the relata should be. There is exactly one intrinsic nature any of us knows from the inside, and it's experience. Panpsychism fills the blanks with the only material available. Consciousness is not produced by matter at some threshold of complexity; it is what matter is like from within, all the way down, in forms so simple that the word experience is nearly too big for them. Brains don't create it. They arrange it.

The case for this is elimination. Materialism has no account of how experience arises from ingredients that have none; the accounts on offer explain what a brain does, not why doing it feels like anything. Dualism has no account of how two substances touch. Panpsychism refuses the first problem's premise and pays with a problem of its own: how many small experiences become the one you're having. Nobody has solved that either. But it is the kind of problem you can work on, which is more than can be said for the other two.

## The step the equation won't take

In 1926 Schrödinger wrote down how a quantum system evolves, and it evolves beautifully: smoothly, deterministically, with no gaps. The catch is what evolves. Not a position, not a momentum, but a wave function, which assigns an amplitude to every possible state at once. An electron with no particular location; a spin that is up and down in fixed proportion. This is superposition, and it is not a gap in our knowledge. The interference patterns only come out right if the alternatives are all there.

Then something is measured, and there is one outcome. The equation never does this. Nothing in it selects a result; it takes superpositions and evolves them into larger superpositions, which is what Schrödinger's cat was built to show. The cat was an objection, not a proposal: follow the mathematics into the macroscopic world and you get an animal that is alive and dead in proportion, which is absurd, so something is missing. Ninety years on, it is still missing. Every interpretation of quantum mechanics is an answer to what ends the superposition, and none of them is accepted by everyone.

Von Neumann showed in 1932 that the cut can go anywhere along the chain: the detector, the wire, the screen, the retina, the optic nerve. The predictions come out the same wherever you place it. The one point the chain cannot be pushed past is the observer's own experience, because that is where there is, unarguably, one result. Wigner took this literally in 1961: consciousness causes the collapse. He later gave the idea up, and most physicists now lean on decoherence, where entanglement with the environment stops the alternatives from interfering and nobody needs to be looking. Decoherence explains very well why you never see a cat in superposition. It does not explain why you see this cat rather than that one. The last step is still open, and it is open at exactly the place where Russell's blanks are.

## The join

Two problems, each stopping at the same silence. Physics stops short of what matter is; the wave function stops short of a definite fact. Panpsychism says the missing nature is experience. Wigner said the missing step is experience. Put them together and the intrinsic nature of matter is the thing that turns possible into actual. Not a spectator at the far end of von Neumann's chain, but the substrate the chain is made of. The universe is definite because it is experienced.

Notice that this is no longer quite panpsychism. Many small experiencers, each collapsing what they touch, is decoherence in different words; it adds nothing. What the join needs is one experiencer, a single field, of which the small ones are local excitations. Philosophers call this cosmopsychism. The name is recent. The idea is not.

Schrödinger himself was there before the equation was. In the closing pages of What is Life? he argues that consciousness never shows up in the plural, only in the singular, and that the apparent multiplicity of minds is a trick of perspective; he took the position from the Upanishads and said so. He never believed a mind was needed to end a superposition. He believed there was one mind, on other grounds entirely. I'm suggesting the two beliefs sit closer together than he let them.

## Something rather than nothing

Leibniz asked the question in 1714 and called it the first one. The intuition behind it is hard to shake: nothing needs no explaining, no laws, no initial conditions. It is the simplest possible state of affairs, and yet here is an apparently endless quantity of something. Every instinct for economy I have would have picked nothing.

There is a reply, and it deserves to be stated before I disagree with it. There is exactly one way for there to be nothing and endlessly many ways for there to be something, so a blind draw from the possibilities would almost never come up empty. The reply assumes a draw, and a draw is already something: a space of possibilities and a rule for choosing among them. It explains why something rather than nothing by helping itself to a something.

Here is where the join earns its place. A possible universe is a mathematical object; the laws of physics are a set of relations that hold whether or not anything obeys them. Hawking asked what breathes fire into the equations and makes a world for them to describe. One school answers: nothing does, mathematical existence is the only kind there is, and we are a structure that happens to describe itself. That answer has to say that the difference between being described and being is no difference at all. It doesn't feel like no difference. Something here is being experienced, and that is precisely the fact the equations leave out.

The field is the fire. What it adds to the mathematics is the thing panpsychism said was missing from physics and the measurement problem says is missing from the wave function: the fact of being some way, from within, now. A possible world becomes an actual one where it is experienced, and the field is that point everywhere at once. Abstraction becomes concrete at the moment of attention.

What this does not do is answer Leibniz. It moves him. Why is there a field rather than nothing? The argument now has two doors, and they turn out to be the two doors theology found a long time ago: either the field is a brute fact, in which case one unexplained given has been traded for a stranger one, or the field is the kind of thing that cannot fail to exist, whose nature is simply to be. The second door is Aquinas's. I didn't expect to arrive at it by way of a wave function, and I'm not sure that makes it any easier to walk through.

## Echoes

If something like this is true, people should have noticed it before, in whatever vocabulary they had. They did, and the noticing is strikingly uniform.

In Genesis the world is spoken into being, and the refrain that keeps returning is that God saw it. Articulation, then witness. John opens with the Logos, the word and the reason and the pattern, and the pattern becomes flesh: abstraction made concrete is the whole first chapter. The Quran compresses it to a command, kun fa-yakūn, be, and it is. Older than both, the priests of Memphis wrote that Ptah conceived the world in his heart and brought it forth on his tongue.

The Upanishads name a single consciousness, Brahman, and tell you that you are it. Schrödinger read that and stopped looking. The Yogācāra Buddhists called their position mind-only. The Tao Te Ching begins with the nameless as the origin of everything and naming as the mother of the ten thousand things, which is not a bad description of what a measurement does. Aboriginal Australians say the ancestors sang the country into existence, and that it holds because the songs are still sung.

Then the philosophers. Aristotle's unmoved mover is thought thinking itself. Spinoza's one substance has thought and extension as two faces of the same thing. And Berkeley, in 1710, made the argument in this article almost exactly, minus the wave function: to be is to be perceived, and the trees in the park go on existing because God never stops perceiving them. I am not the first to arrive here. I'm not even the first to arrive here from physics; Wheeler spent his last decades on a participatory universe in which observation is what brings the past into being, and coined "it from bit" for it.

Not everyone agrees, which is worth keeping in view. Luria's kabbalah has God contract to make room for a world: creation by withdrawal, not by attention. A theory that only ever finds what it's looking for should distrust itself, and this one especially, since finding patterns across distant things is the pleasure I'm most susceptible to.

So the echoes are not evidence. Scripture is large and the mind is built to see faces. What they show is narrower and, I think, still worth something: that when people reasoned about why there is a world, in languages that never touched, the same shape of answer kept coming out. A mind, prior, attending.

## What the argument doesn't give you

The physics is compatible with the field. It is not evidence for it. An always-present experiencer makes exactly the same predictions as no experiencer, because it is always present; nothing in the laboratory changes depending on whether anyone is looking. The quantum eraser experiments say as much: interference comes and goes with whether a record exists, not with whether anyone has read it. Until an experiment turns on the presence of experience itself, the join is philosophy, and should be introduced as such.

It gives you one subject, not a person. What comes out the far end is Spinoza's God, or Berkeley's, or Schrödinger's Brahman: a single field of experience, prior to everything, in which the universe is definite. Not one that answers prayers or knows your name. Whether that deserves the word God is a question about what you need the word for.

And it inherits a problem from the tradition it belongs to. Panpsychism has to explain how many small experiences combine into one. Cosmopsychism has to explain the reverse: how one field is, right now, separately, you and me. Nobody has done it. Schrödinger's answer was that the separateness is an illusion, which is either the solution or a refusal to give one.

## The shift

Stop asking what the universe is made of. The equations answer that as far as it can be answered, and they answer it as relations, with the relata left blank. Ask instead what makes the equations true of something.

If the answer is nothing, then we are a mathematical structure, and the fact that it is like anything to be one is a mistake we are all making together. If the answer is something, it is the thing every tradition was pointing at, and physics stops one step short of it on purpose, because Galileo told it to.
